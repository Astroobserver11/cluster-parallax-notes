# Reasons Why the 24-inch High-Cadence Crab Optical Photometry Experiment Has Not Been Prioritized

**Date:** 2026-10-25

## Summary
The proposed experiment—high-cadence (100–300 fps) optical photometry of the Crab pulsar on a 24-inch telescope with an ordinary CCD, searching for periodic signals, harmonics, or signatures that could support plasma-universe/relaxation-circuit models over the standard rotating neutron star—has not been pursued at scale for both scientific and practical reasons.

## Scientific Reasons
- **Existing high-time-resolution data already constrain the signal tightly.** Instruments such as Aqueye (1.8 m Copernico), Iqueye (3.5 m NTT), IMONY (3.8 m Seimei), OPTIMA, TRIFFID, and STJ arrays have produced microsecond-resolution optical light curves, single-pulse detections, and precise optical–radio phase lags (~200–320 μs). Fourier analyses recover the expected harmonics of the ~30 Hz spin frequency that arise from the non-sinusoidal pulse shape; they do not show residual power at incommensurate frequencies or other anomalies requiring a different clock mechanism.
- **Multi-wavelength consistency.** Radio, optical, X-ray, and gamma-ray pulses are phase-locked to the same period (with understood lags). Spin-down, glitches, polarization, and magnetospheric models all fit the rotating-neutron-star lighthouse picture at high precision. No clear residual remains that a relaxation-circuit interpretation is required to explain.
- **Low incremental scientific return.** Repeating the measurement on a modest aperture with a general-purpose camera is unlikely to reveal new physics unless a statistically significant, unexplained residual appears. Such a residual has not been reported in the existing literature.

## Practical Reasons
- **Photon budget.** Scaling from Aqueye/Iqueye net pulsed rates indicates ~50–120 photoelectrons s⁻¹ on a 24-inch before filter and QE losses. Nebula background is comparable or larger even with a small aperture, making the measurement photon-starved relative to larger professional telescopes.
- **Instrumental efficiency.** Professional programs use purpose-built photon-counting photometers on 1–4 m+ telescopes for higher SNR in less time. Ordinary CCDs at 100–300 fps face readout, noise, and data-volume challenges that specialized detectors avoid.
- **Observational overhead.** Maine winter visibility limits useful hours per night; absolute timing to radio ephemerides requires GPS/rubidium-level synchronization; background subtraction and atmospheric effects add systematic floors.
- **Amateur precedent is limited to demonstration.** Stroboscopic or high-speed camera recoveries of the basic pulse profile exist on smaller instruments, but they have not accumulated the long, precisely timed series needed for high-significance harmonic searches aimed at alternative physics.

## Conclusion
The experiment is feasible as a technical demonstration or educational exercise, but the combination of already-strong constraints from professional datasets and the modest photon rates on a 24-inch aperture means it is not a high-priority path for testing or challenging the standard model. A clear, unexplained residual in high-quality optical timing would be required to change that assessment.
